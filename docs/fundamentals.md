# Fundamentals

This document explains the ideas behind `latticegen`: how a periodic lattice
is described as a scalar field, the three ways the field is built (Fourier
synthesis, distance fields, TPMS), how a field becomes a printable mesh, and
what matters when that mesh goes to a DLP printer. It is written for someone
who knows basic linear algebra and has seen a Fourier series; no
crystallography background is assumed. The companion [usage guide](usage.md)
covers the API.

## 1. Lattices as level sets of periodic fields

A lattice structure is a solid that repeats in three directions. Rather than
building it from explicit cylinders and spheres, `latticegen` describes the
solid implicitly: a smooth scalar field `f(r)` with the periodicity of the
lattice is sampled on a grid over one unit cell, and the solid is the region
where the field exceeds a threshold,

    solid = { r : f(r) >= t }.

This is a *level set* (or implicit surface) representation. It has three
consequences that drive the whole design:

* **Density is one number.** Raising or lowering `t` grows or shrinks the
  solid everywhere at once, so a target volume fraction is reached by picking
  the right threshold, not by re-drawing geometry.
* **Junctions are free.** Where struts meet, their fields simply add or the
  distance simply takes the minimum; there is no boolean union to compute
  and nothing can leak or self-intersect.
* **Grading is a change of threshold.** Letting `t` vary slowly in space
  produces a density gradient while the lattice geometry stays the same.

The price is that the solid boundary is only known to the resolution of the
sampling grid (`N` samples per cell edge), and that the mesh comes out of
marching cubes with many small triangles. Both are dealt with later.

## 2. Bravais cells and the reciprocal lattice

A **Bravais lattice** is the set of all points `n1 a1 + n2 a2 + n3 a3` for
integers `n_i`, where `a1, a2, a3` are the lattice vectors. `latticegen`
stores them as the rows of a 3 x 3 matrix `A`, so that **fractional
coordinates** `u` in `[0,1)^3` map to cartesian coordinates by `r = u A`.
Everything inside the package (strut endpoints, sampling grids) lives in
fractional coordinates; the matrix `A` carries the size, the aspect ratio
and any shear. A cubic cell of edge `a` is `A = a I`; a tetragonal cell
stretches the third row; a general cell is built from the six
crystallographic parameters `(a, b, c, alpha, beta, gamma)`.

The **reciprocal lattice** is spanned by the vectors `b_j` defined by
`a_i . b_j = 2 pi delta_ij`, i.e. `B = 2 pi (A^-1)^T` (rows `b_j`). Every
function with the periodicity of the lattice can be written as a sum of
plane waves whose wave-vectors are reciprocal-lattice vectors

    G = h b1 + k b2 + l b3,     (h, k, l) integers.

The triple `(h k l)` is the familiar Miller index. This is the basis of the
Fourier method in section 4; the distance-field and TPMS methods only need
`A`.

## 3. Strut skeletons

Truss-like lattices (octet, bcc, diamond, ...) are defined by a **skeleton**:
a set of straight struts, each a pair of fractional endpoints `(p, q)`,
listed once per unit cell. Struts that are lattice translates of each other
are the same strut, so the package canonicalises every segment (midpoint
wrapped into the cell, direction sign fixed) and keeps one copy. This is
what allows a topology to be written down by hand or generated by a
nearest-neighbour rule without worrying about double counting.

The built-in topologies and their essential numbers (cubic cell of edge `a`):

| name | struts / cell | strut length | node connectivity | description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sc | 3 | a | 6 | cell edges |
| bcc | 8 | 0.866 a | 8 | corners to body centre |
| fcc | 12 | 0.707 a | 12 / 4 | corners to face centres |
| bccz | 9 | 0.866 a, a | 10 | bcc + vertical edges |
| fccz | 13 | 0.707 a, a | 14 | fcc + vertical edges |
| fbcc | 20 | 0.707 a, 0.866 a | 20 | fcc + bcc |
| octet | 24 | 0.707 a | 12 | fcc nodes, all nearest-neighbour bonds |
| diamond | 16 | 0.433 a | 4 | tetrahedral bonds of the diamond cubic structure |
| kelvin | 24 | 0.354 a | 4 | edges of the space-filling truncated octahedra |

Connectivity matters mechanically (Maxwell's criterion: 3-D frames with
node connectivity of 12 or more, like the octet, are stretching-dominated
and stiff; 4- to 8-connected ones bend) and geometrically: the more struts
meet at a node, the larger the junction bulge discussed below.

Two skeletons can be added (`bcc + sc`), and custom skeletons can be built
from any list of segments, or from nodes plus bond vectors. Isolated points
(spheres) are supported too.

## 4. Fourier synthesis

### 4.1 Plane-wave expansion

Any real function with the periodicity of the lattice is

    f(r) = sum_G  c_G  exp(i G . r),     c_{-G} = conj(c_G).

Truncating the sum to `|G| <= G_max` gives a band-limited, infinitely
smooth field. `latticegen` enumerates all `(h k l)` with `0 < |G| <= G_max`
(the `G = 0` term is a constant and is irrelevant for a level set) and
evaluates the sum on an `N^3` grid with one inverse FFT: coefficient `c_G` is
placed at array index `(h mod N, k mod N, l mod N)`. This is exact provided
`N > 2 max|h|`, otherwise high frequencies alias; the package raises an
error in that case.

### 4.2 Coefficients from a motif: the structure factor

The coefficients come from crystallography's structure factor. A point
"atom" of weight `w` at fractional position `x` contributes
`w exp(-2 pi i (h,k,l) . x)`. A straight strut from `x1` to `x2` is a
uniform line of such atoms; integrating along it gives

    c_G(strut) = w  exp(-i G . r_mid)  sinc( G . d / 2 ),     d = r2 - r1,

where `sinc(x) = sin(x)/x`. Finally every contribution is multiplied by a
Gaussian form factor

    exp( - sigma^2 |G|^2 / 2 ),

which is the Fourier transform of a Gaussian of width `sigma`: it smears the
line skeleton into a tube of soft radius `sigma` and, crucially, damps the
high-frequency terms so the truncation at `G_max` does not ring. The
resulting field is large near the skeleton and small far away, so
`f >= t` is a tube around every strut.

Two parameters control the result:

* `sigma`, the smearing radius, in cell length units. Roughly, the field's
  cross-section is a Gaussian of that width, so the strut radius reached
  at a given density is *not* `sigma`; it is set by the threshold. `sigma`
  controls how round and how soft the struts are and how much they blend at
  junctions. Too small a `sigma` for the chosen `G_max` leaves ringing:
  side-lobes of the truncated series can rise above the threshold and
  produce tiny floating blobs (the package's connectivity check detects
  them, section 13).
* `G_max`, the cutoff, in units of `2 pi / a`. The built-in topologies carry
  tuned pairs (e.g. octet: `sigma = 0.06 a`, `G_max = 6.5`; kelvin: `0.07 a`,
  `5.5`).

### 4.3 Junction pile-up and node compensation

Because the fields of the struts add, `f` at a node where twelve struts meet
is much larger than at mid-strut, and the level set inflates there. Measured
with a distance transform on the octet at 30 % density, the ratio of the
inscribed radius at a node to the strut radius is

| field | node / strut radius |
|---|---|
| Fourier, uncompensated | 2.45 |
| Fourier, `node_comp = 0.5` | 1.44 |
| distance field, sharp union | 1.43 |
| distance field, `smooth = 0.02 a` | 1.97 |
| distance field, `smooth = 0.05 a` | 3.41 |

Node compensation adds a negative-weight Gaussian ("anti-atom") at every
node, wider than the strut smearing (`1.7 sigma` by default) so it carves
the whole bulge rather than a dimple. Its weights are found by a short
fixed-point iteration so that the field at the node becomes
`alpha f(mid-strut) + (1 - alpha) f(node)`. With `alpha = 0.5` the octet
junction matches a sharp cylinder union while keeping a fillet; larger
values waist the junction and eventually pinch it off. Anti-atoms also
create a negative dip around each node; at low densities (below about 15 %
for the octet) that dip can isolate the ring of the surrounding struts into
floating fragments, so compensated Fourier fields should be checked with
`connectivity_check` and used at moderate densities.

## 5. Distance fields

### 5.1 Exact distance to the skeleton

The second method skips Fourier space altogether. For each grid point the
package computes the shortest Euclidean distance `d(r)` to the strut
skeleton, minimised over all periodic images of the struts (the 27
neighbouring cells suffice for any connected cubic topology). The distance
from a point to a segment is the textbook formula: project onto the
segment axis, clamp the parameter to `[0, 1]`, take the norm of the
residual. The field handed to the meshing stage is `f = -d`, so that
`f >= t` is exactly `d <= r` with `r = -t`: the solid is the set of points
within `r` of the skeleton, i.e. a union of cylinders of radius `r` with
spherical caps, joined at the nodes. Struts have *exactly* circular
cross-sections and a known radius, which is what one wants when the part
is later pyrolysed or when a stress calculation assumes cylinders.

Cost is `N^3 x (number of segment images)` point-segment distances. The
implementation processes the grid in cubic blocks and, for each block,
skips every segment image whose bounding box is farther than an upper bound
on `d` (obtained from a coarse exact pass plus the Lipschitz constant of
the distance, which is 1). On an octet at `N = 96` this takes a few
seconds.

### 5.2 Smooth minimum (fillets)

The hard minimum gives re-entrant sharp corners where cylinders intersect.
Replacing it by the exponential smooth minimum

    d_k = -k ln( sum_i exp(-d_i / k) )

rounds the junctions with a fillet of size about `k` (`smooth` in the API).
Unlike the Fourier sum, the pile-up is *bounded*: where `n` struts meet the
effective distance drops by at most `k ln n`, i.e. `~2.5 k` for the
twelve-connected octet node. It is still a strong effect relative to the
strut radius: the table above shows the node radius ratio rising from 1.43
to about 2.0 at `k = 0.02 a` and 3.4 at `k = 0.05 a` for struts of radius
`~0.085 a`. Keep `k` around 10 to 30 % of the strut radius if uniform
sections matter.

The log-sum-exp is evaluated in a numerically stable streaming form
(subtracting the running minimum) so `k` can be small without overflow.

## 6. Triply periodic minimal surfaces

TPMS lattices are not strut-based. A minimal surface such as the gyroid
divides space into two interpenetrating labyrinths; the standard way to
work with it is a low-order trigonometric approximation of its level-set
function, for example (with `X = 2 pi x / a` etc.)

| surface | approximant |
|---|---|
| gyroid | `sin X cos Y + sin Y cos Z + sin Z cos X` |
| Schwarz P (primitive) | `cos X + cos Y + cos Z` |
| Schwarz D (diamond) | `sin X sin Y sin Z + sin X cos Y cos Z + cos X sin Y cos Z + cos X cos Y sin Z` |
| Schoen I-WP | `2 (cos X cos Y + cos Y cos Z + cos Z cos X) - (cos 2X + cos 2Y + cos 2Z)` |
| Neovius | `3 (cos X + cos Y + cos Z) + 4 cos X cos Y cos Z` |

plus Lidinoid, Fischer-Koch S and split-P. These are themselves short
Fourier series, so they fit the same level-set machinery. Two kinds of
solid are used:

* **Network** solid, `F >= t`: one labyrinth thickened. At 50 % density
  (`t = 0`) it is the classical half-space gyroid; at lower density the
  network thins and, for some surfaces (Schwarz P below about 20 %),
  pinches off into disconnected pieces.
* **Sheet** solid, `|F| <= t`: a wall of finite thickness following the
  surface. Both labyrinths stay open, which is excellent for resin drainage
  and gives a stiff, smooth structure. The package implements it as the
  field `-|F|`, so the common convention `f >= t` still holds.

TPMS fields sampled in a non-cubic cell are simply stretched; the
`periods` argument repeats the surface several times per cell.

## 7. Density, isovalue and calibration

The **relative density** (volume fraction) `rho` of the solid is the
quantity a designer specifies. For a field sampled on a fine grid, the
fraction of samples with `f >= t` estimates the volume of `{f >= t}`, so
the threshold is the `(1 - rho)` quantile of the sampled values. `UnitField`
caches the sorted samples and interpolates the quantile function; this is
what makes graded densities (section 9) cheap.

There is a subtlety. Highly symmetric fields have many voxels with
*identical* values (an octet distance field on a 48^3 grid has large
plateaus), so the quantile function has flat steps and two nearby
densities can map to the very same isovalue. The marching-cubes mesh, which
interpolates between grid values, has a volume that varies smoothly with
`t`, so its density does not match the requested one exactly (errors up to
about 1 % absolute at `N = 48` were observed). For scalar and per-cell
densities `latticegen` therefore **calibrates** the isovalue: it meshes one
closed unit cell, measures its volume, and iterates the isovalue with a
secant method until the meshed density matches `rho` to 2 x 10^-4. A few
single-cell meshings cost a fraction of a second. Callable (continuously
graded) densities use the quantile mapping directly, which is accurate to
about the plateau width.

For distance fields the isovalue *is* minus the strut radius, so the
radius reached at a given density is known exactly. For reference, at
`N = 64`:

| topology | r/a at 10 % | 20 % | 30 % | 40 % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| octet | 0.047 | 0.068 | 0.088 | 0.104 |
| bcc | 0.071 | 0.109 | 0.134 | 0.160 |
| diamond | 0.071 | 0.109 | 0.134 | 0.160 |
| kelvin | 0.066 | 0.099 | 0.126 | 0.151 |

(bcc and diamond have the same total strut length per cell, 6.93 a, hence
the same radii at low density where `rho ~ pi r^2 L / a^3`.)

## 8. From field to mesh

### 8.1 Marching cubes

The level set is extracted with the marching-cubes algorithm (Lewiner's
topologically consistent variant from scikit-image): each grid cube whose
corner values straddle the isovalue receives a small patch of triangles
whose vertices are placed by linear interpolation along the cube edges. The
result is a closed surface of the periodic solid inside the block, faithful
to within a fraction of a voxel, but with roughly two triangles per
crossed voxel face and hence very many small triangles.

Because a voxel edge commonly carries an isovalue crossing exactly at a
grid point (symmetric fields again), the level is nudged by `10^-6` of the
field range so that no vertex coincides with a sample and no zero-area
triangles are produced.

### 8.2 Assembling a block and closing it with flat caps

An `(nx, ny, nz)` block is assembled by tiling the unit-cell field, so the
sample grid has `n N + 1` points along each axis (the last plane is the
periodic closure of the last cell). The block is padded on all six sides
with one layer of a very negative value. Marching cubes then automatically
generates the *caps*: the triangles between the last real plane and the
padding plane. Their vertices are subsequently clamped onto the exact
bounding planes, which turns them into perfectly flat faces and is
equivalent to an isosurface plus isocaps but without any 2-D contouring.

### 8.3 Slab streaming and welding

To bound memory the block is processed one slab of `slab_cells` cell
layers at a time. Adjacent slabs share a sample plane; the plane is filled
with the *same* arithmetic in both slabs so the two copies of every vertex
on it are bitwise identical. Vertices are then merged by exact matching of
their float32 bit patterns, which is both fast (an integer sort) and safe
(there are no false merges). Slab streaming thus produces exactly the same
mesh as a one-shot extraction, and the result is watertight: every edge has
exactly two faces. Face orientation is flipped once at the end so normals
point outward (positive signed volume).

### 8.4 Coordinates and units

All processing happens in grid index units. At the end vertices are
divided by `N` and multiplied by `A`, so the mesh is in the cartesian
units of the cell. Use millimetres for printing.

## 9. Graded and heterogeneous blocks

Because density is a threshold, a spatially varying density is a spatially
varying threshold `t(r) = Q(1 - rho(r))`, where `Q` is the unit field's
quantile function. `mesh_stack` supports four forms:

* a scalar (uniform);
* an `(nx, ny, nz)` array: one density per cell, so the threshold is
  piecewise constant and every cell is exactly at its target;
* `rho(zeta)`, a function of the fractional height `zeta = z / (nz |a3|)`:
  a continuous gradient along the build direction;
* `rho(x, y, z)`, a function of cartesian position: any 3-D gradation
  (radial, distance to a surface, from a simulation result...).

The lattice geometry is preserved exactly; the density is met in the
cell-averaged sense (exactly in the limit of slow variation). With
continuous grading the threshold is evaluated at every sample of every
slab, so the requirement that slab joints be bitwise identical becomes a
requirement that the user's function be *elementwise and deterministic*
(the same input value gives the same output, whatever the array shape),
which any expression built from numpy arithmetic satisfies.

A `cell_map` array selects, per cell, which of several fields (same `N`)
to use, so a block can have an octet skin and an fcc core, or a stiff
lattice under a load pad and a light one elsewhere. Two fields connect
only where **both are solid on the shared cell face**. The face pattern of
a strut lattice is its set of nodes and struts on that plane; fcc struts
are a subset of the octet's, so fcc inside octet is seamless, and any
topology is compatible with itself at a different density. An octet shell
around a gyroid core, on the other hand, comes out as two separate solids.
`mesh_stack` warns when a mesh has more than one component.

## 10. Instancing and seam-consistent simplification

For a *homogeneous, uniform-density* block the mesh itself is periodic,
so there is no need to march the whole block. `mesh_instanced` meshes one
unit cell open at its faces (body) plus the six cap patterns, replicates
them with integer offsets and welds exactly, as above. Time and memory are
independent of the block size.

The real value is that the unit pieces can be simplified *before*
replication, provided the interfaces where pieces meet stay consistent:
the periodic face loops of the body, the cap rims and the chains along the
cube edges. Two levels are offered.

* **Frozen seams.** The interior of the body is decimated or remeshed with
  every boundary vertex frozen. Robust, but the seams keep their
  marching-cubes density.
* **Seam-consistent subsampling** (`seam_spacing`). All seam curves are
  collected on the *quotient* cell, where opposite faces are identified,
  and decomposed into chains between breakpoints (vertices of degree other
  than 2, or where the set of bounding planes the vertex lies on changes).
  Each chain is subsampled greedily at arc spacing `h` (given in the cell
  length unit and converted to voxels internally). Because the
  decision is made once per quotient vertex, the body, the caps and all
  periodic partners inherit identical keep/drop choices, so they still
  match after replication. Dropped seam vertices are collapsed along their
  chain onto the nearest kept one; interior vertices closer than `0.4 h`
  to the seam are snapped as well (removing the sliver strip the collapse
  would leave); degenerate faces and zero-thickness fins are removed, and
  any snap that would create a non-manifold edge is reverted. Body and
  caps are then isotropically remeshed against the coarse frozen seams.
  The result has near-uniform triangles everywhere, with 10 to 50 times
  fewer faces than the raw block (a 6 x 6 x 6 octet block at `N = 96`
  came out at 0.7 M faces instead of 20 M).

Coarsening the seams inscribes the strut cross-sections at every cell
interface and the interior remesh smooths slightly, so the volume drops by
an `O(h^2)` amount. `mesh_instanced` measures the simplified unit-cell
density and iterates the isovalue (secant) until it matches the target.

Isotropic remeshing keeps a ring of vertices every edge length along every
strut, although a straight tube carries no information between its ends.
`remesh="decimate"` replaces the interior remesh by quadric edge collapse
(Garland-Heckbert) with a low triangle-quality bound, so triangles are free
to stretch along the struts; the face budget is that of the isotropic mesh
divided by `anisotropy` (default 4). The seams are protected by a very
large boundary weight rather than by a selection, because pymeshlab's
selected-only collapse loses its selection as it proceeds and stalls far
above the target; the canonical key set of the boundary is verified
unchanged afterwards. On a 1 mm octet at 20 % with 0.05 mm seams this
gives ~1 350 faces per cell instead of ~6 500.

## 11. Explicit strut meshes: prisms and hull nodes

Every field-based route spends triangles in proportion to surface area
over voxel size squared, and remeshing can at best bring that to about one
triangle per (edge length)^2 of surface. Neither exploits the one thing a
strut lattice is made of: straight cylinders. A cylinder is fully described
by two end rings; nothing between them needs a vertex. `mesh_struts`
therefore builds the solid directly from the skeleton (section 3), with no
field, no marching cubes and no welding.

**Prisms.** A strut of radius `r` becomes a regular `n`-gon prism (`sides`,
default 6): two rings of `n` vertices and `2n` triangles, whatever its
length. `r` is the circumradius of the polygon; the thinnest dimension is
`2 r cos(pi/n)` (0.87 of the diameter for a hexagon, 0.71 for a square).
Both rings of a strut use the same orthonormal frame, so the prism is not
twisted.

**Pull-back.** Two cylinders of radius `r` whose axes meet at an angle
`theta` overlap up to a distance `r cot(theta/2)` from the node along each
axis. The rings are therefore placed at

    d = 1.02 r / tan(theta_min / 2)

from the node centre, `theta_min` being the smallest angle between struts
meeting there (octet: 60 degrees, `d = 1.73 r`; bcc: 70.5 degrees,
`1.41 r`; simple cubic: 90 degrees, `r`). A vertex of a neighbouring ring
projects onto the axis at most `d cos(theta) + r sin(theta)`, which is at
most `d` exactly when `d >= r cot(theta/2)`: with this pull-back every ring
plane is a supporting plane of the set of all rings of the node.

**Hull nodes.** The node is the convex hull of its rings (12 x 6 = 72
points for an octet node). Because each ring plane supports the point set,
the ring polygon appears as one facet of the hull; that facet is removed
and the prism attaches to the opening, using the same vertex indices. The
remaining hull triangles are the node's skin: `2 k n - 4 - k (n - 2)` of
them for `k` struts (92 for the octet node), with the outer vertices at
`sqrt(d^2 + r^2)` from the centre, i.e. `2.0 r` for the octet and less than
the `2.3 r` of a filleted distance field at `smooth = 0.02 a`. An octet
cell costs `4 x 92 + 24 x 12 = 656` triangles; a marching-cubes cell at
`N = 80` costs about 120 000. Nodes whose incident rings are identical up to
translation share one hull computation, so a 20 x 20 x 20 block (34 000
nodes) needs a handful of hulls. A node with a single strut (a strut that
reaches the block boundary and stops, as bcc corners do) is closed by a
flat `n`-gon cap.

**Strut-sized (union) nodes, the default.** The hull has to enclose rings
that sit `d` from the centre, so its outer vertices reach `sqrt(d^2 + r^2)`,
twice the strut radius for the octet. `node="union"` instead builds one
convex piece per strut: its prism continued from the ring to the node
centre, closed behind the centre by a dome of the polygon's inradius
`r cos(pi/n)`. A point closer than 90 degrees to some strut and within that
inradius of the centre lies in that strut's prism, and any other point lies in
the dome of a strut pointing away from it. So the pieces together contain the ball of the
inradius, and no point of the node is farther than `r` from the skeleton.
The node is the union of the pieces, a mesh boolean done by `manifold3d`
(Manifold's WebAssembly build in the browser app). Rings sit at
`max(d, 1.1 r)` so that every ring stays one facet of the union, and the
ring facets are removed exactly as for hull nodes. Each distinct node is
computed once and everything scales with `r`, so the closed-form density below
still holds. The cost is triangles, because the prisms intersect along polylines.
An octet bulk cell has 1287 triangles (hull: ~700), bcc 336, kelvin 1345.
At the same density the struts come out slightly thicker
(octet 20 %: `r` 0.0752 vs 0.0741 mm for 1 mm cells), because the nodes hold less
material. Overlap limits also relax, since union nodes extend only `r`; fcc +
bcc combined reaches a bulk density of 0.63 instead of 0.20 (octet: 0.83 vs 0.79).

Vertices are shared by index between prisms and nodes, so the mesh is
watertight by construction and no welding is involved (union nodes match
their ring vertices to the boolean's output within `1e-5 r`).

**Density.** The solid volume of one period is in closed form:

    V(r) = sum_struts A_n(r) (L - d_a - d_b) + sum_nodes V_node(r)
         = alpha r^2 - beta r^3,       A_n(r) = (n/2) r^2 sin(2 pi / n)

because `d` and the hull scale linearly with `r`. The strut radius for a
bulk density `rho` is the root of `V(r) / V_cell = rho`, found by
bracketing in microseconds; no meshing or calibration loop is needed and
the meshed volume equals the formula to float32 rounding. The formula, and
the mesh, are valid while primitives that share no node stay apart:
`r_max` is the smallest of half the nearest node distance over
`R1 = sqrt(d1^2 + 1)` (1 for union nodes), the node to non-incident strut distance over
`R1 + 1`, half the nearest non-adjacent strut distance, and the
radius at which `d_a + d_b` reaches a strut length (`d1 = d / r`). For the
octet this is `r = 0.174 a`, a bulk density of 0.79; higher values raise a
`ValueError` (fcc + bcc combined, whose struts pass close to each other,
tops out at 0.20).

**Block boundary.** The block keeps every strut whose two nodes lie in the
closed box `[0, n a]^3`: surface nodes are complete and there are no half
struts, so the part is about `2 r` larger than `n a` and its relative
density is a little above `rho` (extra boundary struts; 3 % on a 20-cell
block, 30 % on a 2-cell one). The field meshers instead cut the solid at
the box faces.

**Rounded nodes.** `node="round"` adds a sampled sphere of radius
`sqrt(d^2 + r^2)` to the hull input (points that would lie beyond a ring
plane are dropped so the openings survive): the node becomes a rounded
convex bulge. What a convex hull cannot make is a *concave* fillet in the
valley between two struts, the shape the smooth-minimum distance field
produces; the hull bridges that valley with a flat web. For struts of a
few pixels the difference is below one pixel; for thick struts where the
fillet matters, use `sdf_field(..., smooth=k)` with `mesh_instanced`.

## 12. Simplification, remeshing and repair

For graded or mixed blocks, or for a block that has already been built,
three standard mesh operations are provided through `pymeshlab`
(MeshLab's filters):

* **Quadric edge-collapse decimation** (Garland and Heckbert). Every vertex
  carries a quadric error matrix summarising the planes of its incident
  faces; edges are collapsed in order of the error the collapse would
  introduce, to a target face count. Topology preservation is enabled so
  struts cannot merge or split, and a planar-quadric term lets the flat
  caps collapse into a few large triangles. Volume changes are typically
  below 0.3 % and the mesh stays watertight. This is the tool for print
  files: `simplify(edge_length=h)` derives the face budget from the
  surface area (`area / (sqrt(3)/4 h^2)` equilateral triangles).
* **Isotropic explicit remeshing**: repeated edge split / collapse / flip
  and tangential smoothing with re-projection onto the original surface
  yield near-equilateral triangles of a chosen edge length while
  preserving creases above a dihedral angle (the cap rims). Smoothing
  shrinks thin features a little (a few per cent of the volume at coarse
  edge lengths), so this is the choice for finite-element meshes rather
  than for print files.
* **Repair**: duplicate vertices and faces, degenerate faces, non-manifold
  edges, small holes and inconsistent orientation are fixed; the mesh is
  flipped if its signed volume is negative. **Island removal** drops
  disconnected components below a size or volume fraction.

## 13. Feature sizes and connectivity

Two questions decide whether a lattice prints: are the solid features thick
enough, and does everything hang together? `UnitField.feature_sizes` and
`connectivity_check` answer them on the sampled unit cell.

* The **Euclidean distance transform** (EDT) of the solid mask, computed on
  a periodically padded copy, gives at every solid voxel the distance to
  the nearest void. Its value at a strut midpoint is the strut's inscribed
  radius; its value at a node is the node radius; its ridge (local maxima,
  the medial axis) traces the local half-thickness of every feature, and a
  low percentile of the ridge is a robust "thinnest place". The same on
  the void gives the largest pore and the narrowest pore throat, which
  govern resin drainage.
* **Connectivity** is checked with a connected-component labelling of
  each phase, merged across the periodic faces (union-find), and a
  percolation test on a 2 x 2 x 2 tiling: a piece that never reaches the
  tiling boundary is a closed pocket (void) or a floating island (solid).
  A printable lattice needs one percolating solid, no floating islands and
  no enclosed voids; a sheet TPMS legitimately reports two void labyrinths.

## 14. DLP printing considerations

A DLP printer cures resin one layer at a time with a projected image, so
the part is quantised laterally to the **pixel pitch** (typically 20 to
100 um) and vertically to the **layer thickness** (25 to 100 um). The
following are the practical rules the report in `dlp_report` encodes.

* **Resolution of the field.** The voxel of the sampling grid should not be
  coarser than the printer pixel, otherwise the staircase of the level set
  is visible on the struts. `recommend_grid` chooses `N` so that the voxel
  is at most half a pixel. Finer than a quarter pixel buys nothing and
  multiplies the triangle count.
* **Minimum feature.** Struts and walls need at least 3 to 5 pixels of
  width to cure reliably and survive washing; the report converts the
  thinnest solid feature to pixels and layers. Increase the density or the
  cell size, not `N`, to fix it.
* **Overhangs and supports.** Lattices are mostly self-supporting: every
  strut is anchored at both ends and inclined struts print as gradual
  overhangs. Horizontal struts (sc, the pillars of bccz/fccz rotated) are
  the exception; orient the block so that struts are inclined, or accept
  slight sag. Vertical struts of bccz and fccz are strong in the build
  direction, which is also where DLP parts are strongest.
* **Drainage and cleaning.** Uncured resin must leave the part. The pore
  throat should be several pixels wide; enclosed pockets (some TPMS
  network solids at high density, hollow nodes) are unacceptable, and
  large blocks benefit from open labyrinths (sheet gyroid, high
  connectivity). The report flags narrow throats and enclosed voids.
* **Floating pieces.** Anything not connected to the build platform falls
  into the vat and can damage the film. Disconnected mesh components and
  isolated solid blobs in the unit cell are flagged; `remove_islands`
  deletes them from an existing mesh.
* **Caps.** The flat faces of the block are where the part touches the
  platform and where loads are applied; they are produced exactly planar.
* **File size and slicer limits.** Binary STL is 50 bytes per triangle;
  many slicers struggle beyond a few million triangles. Instancing or
  decimation to an edge length of about one pixel brings a block from
  hundreds of megabytes to tens.
* **Shrinkage.** Post-curing shrinks parts by up to a few per cent and
  pyrolysis (for ceramic lattices) by tens of per cent; the report accepts
  a linear shrinkage so that feature sizes are reported for the final
  part. Uniform strut sections (sharp distance-field unions, or Fourier
  fields with node compensation) shrink more evenly than bulged nodes.

## 15. Choosing between the methods

| | Fourier synthesis | distance field | TPMS | explicit struts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| geometry | smooth, organic tubes; blended junctions | exact cylinders, sharp or filleted unions | curved walls or labyrinths | n-gon prisms, convex polyhedral nodes |
| strut radius | set by threshold, not known in closed form | known exactly (`-t`) | wall thickness from EDT | known exactly (input, or closed form from rho) |
| junction bulge | large; fix with `node_comp` | small (sharp) to large (smooth) | none | node vertices at ~2 r; convex only, no fillets |
| ringing / artefacts | possible at small `sigma` or strong `node_comp` | none | none | none |
| cost at N = 96 | milliseconds (FFT) | seconds | milliseconds | no grid; ~20 us per strut |
| triangles per octet cell | ~1 per px^2 after remeshing (~6 000) | same | same | 656 |
| general cells | yes | yes | stretched | yes |
| best for | organic-looking parts, very fast iteration, heterogeneous blends | engineering trusses with fillets, graded blocks, FEA comparison | bone scaffolds, heat exchangers, drainage-critical parts | large uniform truss blocks, thin struts, slicer-friendly files |

For DLP work the explicit strut mesh is the default for uniform truss
blocks (it is the only route whose file size stays practical for
thousands of cells); the distance field with a small fillet (`smooth`
around 0.1 to 0.3 of the strut radius) is the choice when fillets or
gradation matter, and the sheet gyroid for TPMS.
